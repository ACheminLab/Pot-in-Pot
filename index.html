Cooling without electricity

A pot-in-pot cooler uses evaporation to remove heat from its contents. Wind and the evaporating liquid set the cooling power; the pot and contents provide thermal inertia, while heat flows back in from the surroundings. Adjust the conditions and see the temperature evolve.

How the cooler works

Evaporation cools the wet outer surface; heat transfer from the environment opposes that cooling.

Temperature vs time

The temperature follows the energy balance. When the surface dries, the evaporation rate falls and the contents begin to warm again.

Evaporation rate vs time

Wind increases evaporation and humidity suppresses it. In drying-surface mode, the evaporation rate falls as the liquid supply runs out.

Model and interpretation

We treat the cooled contents and pot as one thermal body with heat capacity C. Evaporation removes latent heat at rate Lvṁ. Heat flows back from ambient air through an effective resistance R. With a continuously wet surface, ṁ is constant. When the surface dries, the available liquid is depleted and ṁ decays exponentially.

C dT/dt = −Lvṁ(t) + (Tambient − T)/R
wet surface: Teq = Tambient − R Lvṁ
τ = RC

If the steady-state prediction lies far below ambient, that signals the limit of this simplified model: real evaporation slows as the surface cools and may stop or change regime. Interpret very low predictions cautiously.

Reference: A. Chemin, V. Dit Vehel, A. Caussarieu, N. Plihon, and N. Taberlet, “Heat transfer and evaporative cooling in the function of pot-in-pot coolers,” American Journal of Physics 86, 206–211 (2018). doi:10.1119/1.5016041.
